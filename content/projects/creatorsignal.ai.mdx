<h2 id="problem">1. The problem: data about the last Reel, nothing about the next one</h2>

A creator who posts three or four Reels a week makes the same decision three or four times a week. Instagram's Insights is good at one half of it: reach, views, likes, saves, shares for every Reel they've already posted. The other half, what to post next, is left to the creator.

So they do it by hand. They scroll their own Insights to guess which topics landed. They check YouTube, Reddit and Google to see what their niche is asking about. Then they pick something, mostly on instinct.

AI idea tools look like a shortcut, but they skip the first half. Ask one for a Reel idea and it has no idea which of your topics worked, which hooks your audience responds to, or what you've already posted five times this month. **The ideas fit anyone, which means they fit no one in particular.**

<h2 id="assumption">2. Who it's for, and the riskiest assumption</h2>

The first user is a single creator with a Business or Creator account and enough history to learn from. The system flags any profile with fewer than 15 Reels as an "early profile", because conclusions from a handful of posts are mostly noise.

I haven't interviewed creators yet, and that's the biggest gap in this case study. The design came from my own reading of the problem and several rounds of written critique, not from user research. So the whole product rests on one assumption I haven't tested:

<Callout label="Riskiest assumption">
  A creator will post an idea they didn't come up with themselves, if they can see exactly why it was picked for them.
</Callout>

If that's wrong, nothing downstream matters: not the ranking, not the model, not the explore rate. That's why the first metric in section 7 is whether creators post the pick at all.

<h2 id="bet">3. The bet: one pick, with its working shown</h2>

Most tools in this space give you a dashboard or a list of ten ideas. Both hand the decision back to the creator. I went the other way: one recommendation, with a receipt.

<h2 id="evolution">4. How the design changed before I wrote any code</h2>

The first version of the spec looked impressive and didn't hold up. I put it through several rounds of written technical critique before building, and almost every headline feature changed.

<DataTable
  data='{
    "head":["The first spec said","What it became","Why"],
    "rows":[

      ["A multiplied-out score, plus weights that added up to 90","A weighted sum of five factors, each between 0 and 1","The formula mixed two ideas, and the weights didn’t sum to 100. A sum is easy to explain and to show on a receipt"],

      ["No plan for new creators","An early-profile flag and neutral defaults","A creator with ten Reels doesn’t have enough history to learn from, and the product should say so"],
      ["Always recommend the top idea","85% top idea, 15% a strong runner-up","Always taking the top idea means the system only ever learns about ideas it already likes"],

**The lesson I took from this: the honest version is the stronger version.** A spec that says "one agent, here's exactly why there" survives questions. A spec that says "multi-agent" doesn't.

**The decision I'd defend first is "the model writes, code decides".** It came from a real ambiguity in my own spec: one section said the language model should choose the final idea, another said the explore step should. If the model chooses, the 85/15 split stops meaning anything, because the model can always pull the pick back to its favourite. Making the pick plain Python fixed that, and it made "same input, same pick" true by construction rather than by hoping the model behaves.

<h2 id="build">6. How I built it</h2>

I wrote the spec in three build documents and built it in that order: the data foundation, then the recommendation engine, then the web app and outcome loop. The stack is Python with FastAPI and LangGraph, Postgres on Neon, Qdrant for embeddings (from Voyage AI), Claude Haiku and Sonnet, LightGBM, and a Next.js web app. A few things I'd point a reviewer to:

- **Everything that decides is a pure function.** The score, each factor, the explore pick, the baselines and the Creator DNA all take data in and return a number, with no database or model call inside. That's why most of the 178 tests run with no credentials at all.
- **The agent can't run away.** Its five-round limit is a loop in code, not a line in the prompt. Every model call is injectable, so the loop's stopping, fallback and caching logic is tested without calling a model.
- **Creator DNA is point-in-time.** It only counts Reels posted before the moment it's computed, so it can be rebuilt exactly as it stood on any past date when evaluating old picks.
- **Embeddings carry a version.** If the model, or even the way caption text is assembled, changes, the version changes, so old and new vectors are never compared.
- **Access tokens are encrypted** before they're stored, and a refresh job renews them in their last week before expiry.

<h2 id="metric">7. How I'll know it works</h2>

The point isn't to prove the pipeline produces something plausible. Any language model can do that. The point is to prove it beats simpler ways of choosing an idea. So every run logs what these would have picked from the same ideas:

- **Trending only:** the idea with the strongest trend momentum.
- **Popular only:** the idea with the highest audience demand.
- **Random:** any idea.
- **The creator's all-time best topic.**
- **The creator's most recent hit.**

A sixth, "just ask the model for an idea, with no ranking", is written and tested but not wired into the pipeline yet. It's the one that matters most, because it's the cheap alternative that would make this whole system unnecessary.

<h2 id="next">8. Where it stands, and what's next</h2>

The pipeline, web app and outcome loop are built and tested. The data layer is verified on one real Instagram account with nine Reels, which is itself below the early-profile threshold. No creator has used it, so there is no result to report. What doesn't work yet, plainly:

- **Two factors don't separate ideas.** Research runs once for the whole niche, so audience demand and trend momentum come out the same for every idea in a run. Right now the ranking really separates ideas on fit and novelty only.
- **Expected engagement is a flat 0.5.** The LightGBM model and its training function exist and are tested, but nothing calls them in a live run, so this factor doesn't move the ranking yet.
- **Nothing freezes each pick's features yet.** The database has a table for it, so a future model can't train on information that didn't exist at the time. Nothing writes to it, and that has to change before any model trains.
- **The outcome match is a guess.** It assumes the creator's next Reel is the one the pick was for. The topic-match score shows when that's wrong, but doesn't fix it.
- **Sign-in is beta-grade.** The magic link proves who signed in, but the API doesn't re-check that on every request. Acceptable for invited testers, not for a public product.

What I'd do next, in order:

1. **Talk to creators before onboarding them.** Ten conversations about how they choose their next Reel today, to test the riskiest assumption in words before testing it in data.
2. **Run a small beta.** The setup is sized for 15 to 30 invited creators. Measure adoption first.
3. **Give each idea its own demand and momentum.** It's the biggest gap in the ranking, and the fix is a second, lighter research pass for the shortlisted ideas.
4. **Freeze features at every pick, then switch on the engagement model** once a creator has ten real outcomes.

<h2 id="learnings">9. What I learned</h2>

- **Honest labels are a feature.** "One agent", "composite score", "0.5 until we know" each read as weaker on a slide, and each one held up better under questions.
- **Put judgment in code, words in the model.** Anything that has to be tested, repeated or explained to a user belongs in plain code. The model is good at generating options and explaining a choice, not at being the choice.
- **Build the measurement before the result.** The baselines and outcome loop exist before any user does. That's what will make the first month of data mean something.
- **Next time, users before architecture.** I spent my rigour on the system and none yet on the creator. Show Up started with 30 interviews; this one should have started with ten.
