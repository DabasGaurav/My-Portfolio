<h2 id="problem">1. The problem: a confident sentence with nothing behind it</h2>

I built Proposal Copilot for the consulting round of the Bottoms Up 5.0 case competition with my team, WD Advisors. The case: VCG, a 2,000-consultant firm, has six possible uses for generative AI, ₹8 crore of ideas and ₹3 crore to spend. We had to pick three, and build a working AI agent for one of them.

Proposals are where consultants feel the pressure most. In the case, 1,200 consultants spend about 180 hours a year each on them, roughly 216,000 hours in all. Most of that time goes on the same loop: read a long RFP, hunt through old case studies and CVs for proof, write it up, then have a partner check it.

The obvious product is "AI writes the proposal". The problem with it is just as obvious once you try it. A language model writes *"VCG has previously redesigned lending for a large Indian bank"* with exactly the same confidence whether or not that happened. In a proposal, every sentence is a promise, and a partner who can't tell which sentences are true has to re-check all of them. That isn't saving time.

So the real question wasn't "can AI write a proposal?" It was:

<Callout label="Riskiest assumption">
  A reviewer will trust and approve an AI draft faster than they could write one, if every claim shows where it came from and nothing unsupported can slip through.
</Callout>

<h2 id="why">2. Why proposals, out of six options</h2>

We scored all six of VCG's options on value, ease of building, adoption, risk and strategic fit. Proposal Development made our funded three, and it was the clear choice for the agent for three reasons:

- **It has a real workflow.** Intake, a bid decision, drafting, checking, sign-off. An agent needs steps; a chat window doesn't.
- **It already has human gates.** A practice lead decides whether to bid, and a partner approves the price. The agent could fit around decisions the firm already makes instead of inventing new ones.
- **Its time turns into revenue.** An hour saved on a proposal can go into the next bid. In the case's numbers, it costs ₹1.1 crore to build, returns ₹0.5 crore in year one and ₹0.9 crore a year by year three.

<h2 id="bet">3. The bet: the model writes, rules check, people decide</h2>

Every design choice comes from one rule: **the model only writes.** It never decides whether a sentence is true, whether to bid, or whether the proposal can go out.

<h2 id="walkthrough">4. A run, screen by screen</h2>

Here is the agent on a sample RFP from "ABC Bank", a retail bank in India that wants to redesign its lending process. The RFP asks for proof of more than a 30% improvement in turnaround time. The firm's best real result is 18%. That gap is the point of the demo.

**The bid decision.** Before any writing, the agent checks how much of what the client asks for the firm can actually prove. Of about 20 requirements, four need proof from past work, and the documents prove two. It says so, recommends a review rather than a bid, and waits for a practice lead to decide and give a reason.

**The model earns its place only where I measured it.** My first version used the model for every step. On a small local model, reading the RFP through the model scored bid fit at 0% and left 70 citations pointing at nothing, because the requirements it extracted matched no evidence. Splitting the draft into individual claims hung for 14 minutes. Plain parsing did both jobs instantly and better. So the model writes, and the structure around it is ordinary code.

**"Supported" means consistent, not true.** I was careful with the words in the product. The agent checks a claim against the firm's own documents. If a document is wrong, a claim built on it will pass. The interface and the README both call it evidence checking, not fact-checking.

<h2 id="build">6. How I built it</h2>

I wrote the spec and the acceptance tests first, then built the agent with Claude Code as my pair programmer. It's Python, with a Streamlit interface, keyword search over the firm's documents, and every run saved to a database so it can be reopened and audited. A few things I'd point a reviewer to:

- **The demo was a test before it was a feature.** The firm's documents include an office sustainability report where electricity use fell 35%. When the model claims a 35% lending improvement, the number exists in the corpus, just in the wrong place. The rule only accepts a figure if the words around it match the claim's topic. "18% passes, 35% is caught" was written down before any code, and the test suite checks it on every change.
- **I tried to break my own checks.** An independent audit and a round of adversarial testing found eleven ways to sneak a claim past the verifier. Each one became a fix and a test. The fixes include catching a claim that cites one real source and one invented one, checking a figure whatever verb introduces it, and refusing "other Indian banks" when one of the cited cases is from Southeast Asia.
- **Expectations were written before results.** For five new sample RFPs, I wrote down what the agent *should* do, from the documents alone, before running it once. Then I compared.
- **It reports its own cost.** Each run records tokens, time and cost: about 14,500 tokens and ₹0.16 at paid rates for a full proposal. The cost of this product is integration and reviewers' time, not the model.

<h2 id="next">8. Where it stands, and what's next</h2>

The agent works end to end on synthetic data, and the live demo runs a real model. Running it showed me where it falls short, which is more useful than the demo itself:

- **It blocks true sentences that forget to cite.** In one run, the same fact about the lead partner was supported in one section and blocked in another, depending on how the model attached its citation. Safe, but it creates review work.
- **The model writes far more than the evidence supports.** On the free model, a handful of claims out of about 80 were fully backed. Some of the rest are fine sentences with no citation; some are section headings the checker treats as claims.
- **The bid screen shows a score, not the gaps.** The agent knows which requirements failed and why ("the RFP asks for 30%, the best evidence is 18%"), but the screen only shows "2 of 4". A practice lead deciding on a number is the wrong design.
- **Search is basic, and the company systems are sample files.** The CRM, HR and rate-card inputs are labelled samples, not live connections.

What I'd do next, in order:

1. **Cut the noise.** Attach the citation automatically when a sentence matches a selected passage word for word, and stop checking headings.
2. **Put the gaps on the bid screen**, with the reason for each, so the decision is made on evidence rather than a percentage.
3. **Run the pilot** above with real proposal writers, and measure reviewer time before anything else.

<h2 id="learnings">9. What I learned</h2>

- **Write the failing test before the feature.** "18% passes, 35% is caught" told me what done looked like before the model wrote a word.
- **Use the model where it earns its place, and measure that.** It made the writing better and the structure worse.
- **An honest gap beats a confident sentence.** The most valuable thing the agent produces is the list of things it can't prove.
- **Trust is a design problem.** Reviewers don't need a smarter model; they need to see the working: the requirement, the quote, the source, the verdict.
